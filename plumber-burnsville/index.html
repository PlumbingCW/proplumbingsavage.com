A city built in a hurry, and the plumbing that came with it.

Burnsville incorporated in 1964 and grew from about 2,700 residents in 1960 to nearly 20,000 by 1970, after Interstate 35W arrived. The city was approaching build-out by the late 1990s and fully built by the late 2000s. That means a large share of homes were framed in the 1960s through the 1990s, with plumbing that is now thirty to sixty years old.

Homes from that boom often have cast iron or clay drain lines, copper supply and original water heaters replaced once or twice. Cast iron scales inside and holds grease. Copper can develop pinholes in aggressive water. When a Burnsville home has repeat clogs or a stained ceiling, age is the first suspect.

Hardness to expect.

Burnsville's 2025 water report states hardness of 25 to 26 grains per gallon and advises matching a softener to that figure.

Water from the quarry and the aquifers.

Burnsville's 2025 consumer confidence report says the city draws surface water from Kraemer Quarry and operates 14 wells from 298 to 1,030 feet deep. The report states hardness of 25 to 26 grains per gallon. That is very hard water, and it explains the scale we find in Burnsville tanks and tankless units.

Hard water shortens the life of a tank and scales a faucet cartridge. A water softener sized to the stated hardness protects both, and we cover the choice when we visit.

What Burnsville homes call us about most.

The first call is usually a drain. A main line that clogs after heavy use, or a tub that gurgles when the washer runs, is the signature of a mid-century drain. We start with drain cleaning and, if roots or scale are the cause, move to hydro jetting.

The second is a water heater. With 25 to 26 grains of hardness, a tank installed in the 2000s may already be rumbling and scaling. We compare repair against replacement honestly.

A third is leaks that show up as a stain or a bill. Our leak detection work starts with a meter test and a listening search so we do not open more of the wall than needed.
